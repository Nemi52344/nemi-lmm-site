The opportunity

Every few decades, the way we make things changes completely.

Ford moved the industry with the assembly line in 1913. Toyota did it again with the Production System in 1950. Computing and digitization compounded efficiency from 1990 onward. Each shift moved enormous market share from the companies that adopted the new method to the ones that did not.

Software has already had its AI moment: one person can now do what once took hundreds of engineers years. Hardware has not. It is still normal for a part to take a month to design and a year to tool.

The prize is the whole physical economy, and the constraint is data. Hardware has no internet to scrape. There are no large public datasets, and the knowledge sits in silos inside companies that will never let outsiders train on it.

Physical economy the LMM addresses

A pure software company cannot reach this data. Whoever intends to automate hardware has to be inside the factory.

The plan

Full-stack on day one. End-to-end earned in phases.

Phase 1

Automate eNvision + Engineer

Rendering, CAD, simulation and PLM rebuilt in-house with generative AI inside. Win high-mix, low-volume work where engineering cost dominates, and buy factories to compound data and cash.

Phase 2

Automate Manufacture

A digital-twin ERP that captures the floor and orchestrates it, people first and robots next. Robotics chosen by ROI, not by hype. Then into high-volume, low-mix work.

Phase 3

Open the platform

With design near-autonomous and the factories reconfigurable, the tools and the factories open as a platform for others to build on.
